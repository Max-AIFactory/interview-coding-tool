[00:00:05] I: To start, could you briefly describe your role and what your company does?

[00:00:20] R: I lead business development at a flexibility marketer. We aggregate industrial loads, mostly cold storage and electrolysis pilots, and market them in the balancing markets and, more recently, on the intraday market. Roughly 40 people, founded in 2018.

[00:02:10] I: What has worked well in practice over the last years, and what have you learned?

[00:02:31] R: The technical side works. Our forecasting and optimisation stack is mature, we learned a lot from the first pilots with cold-storage sites in Bavaria. What we underestimated was how long prequalification for the balancing markets takes, and how much bespoke work each customer needs. Every industrial site is different, so the "one platform fits all" idea died in year two.

[00:04:12] I: Where do the revenues come from today?

[00:05:03] R: Still mostly from the balancing energy markets, FCR and aFRR. Intraday optimisation is growing, but the margins are thin. Grid-fee reductions for atypical usage are the third pillar. Honestly, the revenue streams are not yet stable enough for a big industrial player to reorganise production around them.

[00:07:48] I: What are the biggest barriers you face?

[00:08:15] R: Two things that hang together. First, the metering and baseline rules for aggregated loads are still unclear, so grid operators and we argue about every single prequalification. Second, because of that uncertainty, customers hesitate to sign multi-year contracts. Without the contracts we cannot invest in the customer-side hardware. So the regulatory gap blocks market formation, and the missing market volume in turn blocks investment. It is a vicious circle.

[00:11:02] I: Are there other systems or developments that influence this?

[00:11:30] R: The smart meter rollout is the obvious one. Without certified metering at the site, none of this scales. Battery storage is also changing the picture: stand-alone batteries now compete with us in the FCR market and have pushed the prices down.

[00:14:05] I: How do other actors see aggregators? Grid operators, regulators?

[00:14:37] R: It has improved. Five years ago the distribution grid operators saw us as a nuisance that messes with their load profile. Today at least the transmission operators treat us as partners; they need the flexibility. The regulator, the Bundesnetzagentur, listens but moves slowly. [CONFIDENTIAL] We currently have a legal dispute with one specific grid operator about a rejected prequalification, which I cannot discuss in detail. [/CONFIDENTIAL] Overall the direction is positive.

[00:17:12] I: What about resources: capital, people, data?

[00:17:40] R: Capital was never the main issue for us, we are VC-financed. People are: we compete with the big utilities for the same power-system engineers and data scientists. Data access is the real bottleneck; getting quarter-hour data from a grid operator can take months.

[00:20:31] I: Looking at China, is there anything Germany could learn, or the other way round?

[00:21:09] R: I only know the Chinese situation from conference talks. What impressed me were the provincial demand-response pilots in Jiangsu and Guangdong, where the government simply defines a programme and enrolls hundreds of megawatts within a year. [EN-ORIGINAL] That speed is something we could learn from. [/EN-ORIGINAL] On the other hand, our market-based price signals are probably more sustainable in the long run than administratively set compensation.

[00:27:44] I: Anything else you would like to add?

[00:28:02] R: Only that the Verband, our industry association, has become an important forum. [NO-QUOTE] One of the big utilities tried to have us excluded from the working group last year, which caused quite a stir. [/NO-QUOTE] We exchange experiences on prequalification, and that has shortened the learning curve for new entrants considerably. Größere Sichtbarkeit wäre trotzdem wünschenswert, as we would say in German.
