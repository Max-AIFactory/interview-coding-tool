I: Could you describe your role and the work of your unit?

R: I manage the demand-response (需求响应) unit of a provincial grid company. We run the provincial demand-response programme, sign up industrial users and load aggregators, and dispatch them during summer peaks. Our unit was set up in 2021 after the provincial government issued the first implementation plan.

I: How does the programme work in practice, and what have you learned?

R: The province publishes an annual plan with a target capacity, say 3 GW of adjustable load. We enroll industrial users directly, or through aggregators that bundle smaller users. Compensation is set administratively per kilowatt-hour of reduced load. What we learned: direct enrolment of large users works well, but the aggregator segment is young; many of the so-called virtual power plants (虚拟电厂) are still pilots run by state-owned companies.

I: Who are the aggregators, public or private companies?

R: Mixed. The larger platforms are subsidiaries of the grid company or of the big state-owned generators. There are private start-ups, mostly from the energy-services and EV-charging sector, but they depend on us for data and for the dispatch signal. I would say the direction is set by the public side, and private firms fill in the execution.

I: What are the main obstacles?

R: The market is not really a market yet. Compensation comes from a fund that the provincial pricing bureau fills, and the fund is limited, so once the budget is used up, no more events are called. Aggregators therefore cannot build a stable business case. The spot market reform is supposed to change that, but the rules for aggregated load participating in the spot market are still being drafted. Until the rules exist, the legitimacy of aggregators as market participants remains unclear, and until they are legitimate market participants, nobody will invest in them. These two obstacles lock each other.

I: Are there differences between provinces?

R: Large ones. Jiangsu and Guangdong have several years of experience and hundreds of enrolled users. Inland provinces started only last year. [CONFIDENTIAL] Our own province's target for next year has not been published yet, so I cannot quote the number. [/CONFIDENTIAL] Also, some provinces pay per event, others pay a capacity fee, so an aggregator active in several provinces must handle very different rules.

I: How do you and the regulators view aggregators?

R: From the grid perspective they are useful; they bring us load that we could not reach ourselves. The provincial energy bureau supports them in principle. But there is scepticism about whether private aggregators can guarantee the promised load reduction. Trust has to be earned event by event.

I: What about knowledge and expertise?

R: We learn mostly by doing and through the research institutes attached to the State Grid. Universities run projects with us on baseline methods. There is a lot of exchange within the grid company system, less with private firms; they get the published guidelines and that is it.

I: Is there anything China could learn from Germany, or vice versa?

R: The German market-based approach, where flexibility is traded and prices signal scarcity, is what our spot market reform is aiming at, so yes, the mechanism design is interesting. Germany could learn from our speed of scaling programmes once a decision is made. Both sides struggle with metering and baselines, so that could be a common research topic.

I: Thank you. Anything to add?

R: Only that positive side effects are already visible: the summer peak-shaving programme has trained a whole generation of factory energy managers who now understand load flexibility. That knowledge will not disappear even if the programme changes.
